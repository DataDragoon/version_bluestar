The findings worth knowing

the things that were not obvious before reading the source

No retune logic in fabric
ch 01
There is no hardware SPI sequencer and no fabric-level tuning trigger. Every frequency change is NIOS firmware writing AD9361 registers one transaction at a time. The FPGA contributes only the timestamp compare that says when.
rfic_spi_controller absent
ch 01
It exists in the tree and looks like the AD9361 SPI master, but no Qsys system instantiates it — it ships only in the WLAN image. The hosted build uses Altera's generic SPI core instead.
ps_sync is power supply
ch 01
Not phase-shift. It hops the switching regulators through an LFSR frequency list to spread their emissions away from the RF band — directly relevant to the noise floor.
The GPIF bus is half-duplex
ch 02
fx3_gpif[31:0] is one bidirectional bus shared by both directions. Every turnaround costs a dead gap, and arbitration favours TX unless the RX FIFO passes a critical watermark.
Idle does not mean zero
ch 02
When the TX FIFO runs dry the DAC register holds its last value rather than flushing. That is why libbladeRF zero-pads every burst tail in software.
FPGA ignores the metadata flags
ch 02
BURST_START, BURST_END and TX_NOW are never read by any RTL. TX_NOW is smuggled through the timestamp field: the host writes zero, the reader subtracts one, it wraps to all-ones.
No RX overrun status bit
ch 02
The FPGA blinks an LED and nothing else — the counter output is left unconnected and synthesized away. The host infers overrun only from a discontinuity in the header timestamps, so without the metadata format there is no overrun detection at all.
The trigger forges FIFO empty
ch 02
It does not gate samples. Armed-but-unfired makes the reader believe the FIFO is permanently dry — which also lights the underflow LED continuously.

The Module Inventory

Two things that are easy to confuse

who wrote a block, versus where it is wired in

Every module in this chapter can be described on two independent axes, and mixing them up is the fastest way to misread the design.

Axis one — who wrote the IP

Four suppliers contribute code, one directory each under hdl/fpga/ip/:

Nuand
nuand
The company that designs and sells the bladeRF. Their own VHDL — the datapath, the command UART, the timestamp tamers, the arbiter.
Altera
altera
The FPGA chip vendor (bought by Intel in 2015, spun back out as Altera in 2025). Stock library parts that ship with Quartus — PLLs, FIFOs, SPI and I/O controllers, and the NIOS II soft CPU itself.
Analog Devices
adi
Makes the AD9361 transceiver. Supplies axi_ad9361, the core that drives the LVDS link to their own chip.
OpenCores
opencores
A community open-source IP repository. The I²C master came from there; Nuand wrapped it for Avalon.

Axis two — where it is instantiated

Independently of who wrote it, a block sits in one of two places:

  • Inside the Qsys system. It is on the NIOS’s Avalon bus, gets a base address, and may get an IRQ. These are the modules the firmware can read and write.
  • Hand-instantiated in VHDL — in bladerf-hosted.vhd, rx.vhd or tx.vhd. These are wired directly to pins and to each other, and the NIOS cannot address them at all.

Where I got this wrong

An earlier version of this chapter badged some modules “qsys” alongside “nuand” and “altera”, as if all three were vendors. They are not. Qsys is a tool, not a supplier — it is Altera’s system builder that generates the interconnect. Saying a block is “Qsys” says nothing about who wrote it. The badges below now name the vendor only; the section a module appears in tells you where it is wired.

The cross product is real, not theoretical. command_uart is Nuand IP living inside Qsys; rffe_spi is Altera IP inside Qsys; fx3_gpif is Nuand IP outside it; and nios2 — the CPU this whole guide revolves around — is Altera IP, not Nuand’s.

How the fabric is organised

source of truth · bladerf-hosted.qip + bladerf.qip

The FPGA is not a flat pile of modules. It is three clock domains with carefully guarded crossings between them, and almost every block belongs unambiguously to one of them. Get the domains right and the module list explains itself.

The build manifest is authoritative: bladerf-hosted.qip lists exactly which files are compiled in. Anything not in it — and there are some notable absences — is not in your bitstream, whatever else exists in the tree.

Consequence for the radar

Because there is no hardware SPI sequencer and no fabric-level retune logic, every frequency change on this board is software — NIOS firmware writing AD9361 registers one transaction at a time. The FPGA contributes only the timestamp compare that says when, and the pins that carry the bits.

Chapter 02

The Round Trip

One sample, all the way out and all the way back: from a USB bulk transfer, across three clock domains, out of the antenna, and home again. Every module it touches, every wire it rides, and the cycle counts wherever the RTL commits to one.

2048words per transaction
1022FIFO ops per message
3domain crossings

The map

outbound above, inbound below, clock domains as vertical bands

The two directions are near-mirror images, and they share exactly one resource: the 32-bit GPIF bus, which is bidirectional and therefore half-duplex. Everything else is duplicated.

Outbound — USB to the antenna

1

The host hands over a buffer

EP 0x01 OUT · bulk · 512 B (HS) / 1024 B (SS) packets

Samples and control travel on completely separate endpoints — control is 0x02/0x82, samples are 0x01/0x81. The FX3 firmware sets up two AUTO DMA channels and then never touches sample data again: the USB producer socket feeds PIB_SOCKET_3 outbound, and PIB_SOCKET_0 feeds the USB consumer inbound. DMA buffers are packet_size × 8 — 4096 bytes on High Speed, 8192 on SuperSpeed — eleven deep.

libbladeRF enforces the match from its end: every buffer you configure must be a whole multiple of 8192 bytes, or it silently rounds up and warns.

2

Across the GPIF bus

fx3_pclk (in) · fx3_gpif[31:0] (inout) · fx3_ctl[12:0] (inout)

The FX3 is the clock master. The timing constraint sets the pin at 100 MHz; the firmware programs the PIB clock as SYS_CLK/4, which on the SDK default would be 96 MHz. The SDK is not in the repo, so the real figure sits somewhere between those two.

The control bus is asymmetric in a way worth remembering: bits 0–3 are FPGA outputs (the DMA acknowledgements), 4–12 are inputs (enables, idle, and four active-low DMA requests). Bit 9 is unusable because it carries DCLK, which is why the RX-1 request was relocated to bit 12. Only two of the four DMA channels are ever used — the FPGA always acknowledges on RX0 and TX3, so two of the four ack lines are permanently zero.

A transaction moves exactly one DMA buffer and then returns to idle. One 32-bit bus word is one SC16Q11 sample.

PhaseCycles (SS)What happens
IDLE≥ 1arbitration; seeds the word counter

META_WRITE4the 16-byte header shifts into a 128-bit buffer
SAMPLE_WRITE2044payload straight into the TX sample FIFO

Total 2065 cycles for 8192 bytes — roughly 99% bus efficiency. The metadata lives inside the 2048-word buffer rather than on top of it, so enabling it costs four samples and zero extra cycles.

Two behaviours matter for a radar sweep. Arbitration favours TX: RX only pre-empts once its FIFO passes a critical watermark, deliberately, to avoid discontinuities in the transmitted signal. And because the bus is one set of wires, every direction change costs a dead gap — 13 cycles RX→TX, 16 cycles TX→RX.

3

Out of the FIFO, into the DAC

tx_sample_fifo 32→64, 8192 deep · tx_meta_fifo 32→128 · both show-ahead

fifo_reader runs two independent state machines on tx_clock. The meta FSM walks META_LOAD → META_WAIT → META_DOWNCOUNT; the sample FSM walks COMPUTE_ENABLED_CHANNELS → COMPUTE_OFFSETS → READ_SAMPLES. Those first two sample states are one cycle each and exist purely to recompute the channel packing whenever the FIFO runs dry.

Only two fields of the 16-byte header are ever parsed by the FPGA: the 64-bit timestamp, and in packet mode the length. The flags word — BURST_START, BURST_END, TX_NOW — is never read by any RTL; those are host-side bookkeeping. TX_NOW is encoded in the timestamp instead: the host writes zero, the reader subtracts one, it wraps to all-ones, and the comparison short-circuits.

The 64-bit read word unpacks with the sign-extension bits discarded — the FPGA re-derives sign from bit 11:

Bits63:6059:4847:4443:3231:2827:1615:1211:0
FielddiscardQ1discardI1discardQ0discardI0

With both channels enabled that is one FIFO read per sample instant — 1022 reads per 2044-word message, exactly matching the meta FSM downcount. With one channel enabled, a single read serves two consecutive samples.

From the release comparison going true it is 2 tx_clock cycles to data_v, 3 to the AD9361 data register. The −1 baked into the timestamp comparison exists precisely to pay for that pipeline.

Idle does not mean zero

When the FIFO runs dry, when a burst is not yet due, when the trigger is armed but unfired, or when tx_enable drops — data_v goes low and the DAC register simply holds its last value. It does not flush to zero. That is why libbladeRF zero-pads the tail of every burst in software, and why the metadata contract requires the last samples of a discontinuity to be (0+0j).

One more surprise here: the multi-board trigger does not gate samples at all. It forges the FIFO’s empty flag, so armed-but-unfired makes the reader believe the FIFO is permanently dry. A side effect is that an armed, unfired TX lights the underflow LED continuously. Once it fires, samples flow immediately at whatever the current time is, because the burst’s scheduled timestamp was already consumed while waiting.

The crossing

8 LVDS pairs each way · 6 data lanes, frame, clock

This is the one part of the chain the FPGA does not define. The AD9361 is always in 2R2T LVDS mode on this board, so the framing never changes regardless of how many channels you enable.

The clock chain follows from that geometry: DATA_CLK = 4 × fs, and the factor-4 DDR deserializer halves it, so l_clk = 2 × fs. At a 10 Msps sweep that is a 40 MHz link clock and a 20 MHz fabric clock. The TX side has no clock of its own — it is regenerated from the RX pin through a shared PLL, so the AD9361’s DATA_CLK is the single clock source for the entire radio datapath.

Frame lock is self-checking: every 4-slot frame nibble must be the bitwise inverse of the previous one, which holds only if the period is exactly 8 UI. That check drives the core’s status bit.

One detail that cancels out but will confuse anyone reading registers: the AD9361 has I/Q swap enabled on both ports, so what the RTL calls “I” is the part’s Q. Deliberate, and symmetric end to end.

Inbound — antenna to USB

4

Through the ADI core

ad9361_adc_{i0,q0,i1,q1} · each carries enable, valid, data[15:0]

Twelve l_clk cycles of countable pipeline sit between the deserializer and the fabric: frame decode, 48-bit assembly, data formatting, DC filter, IQ correction. All four channels are bit-identical, so there is zero relative skew between I and Q or between channels. Channel 0 trails channel 1 by one cycle, because it waits in the assembly register for its partner.

valid arrives once every two l_clk cycles — the same rate whether one channel or two is enabled. What changes is how many streams carry payload on it.

The enable line is a trap: it is a static configuration register bit, not a per-cycle data qualifier. It means “this channel is configured”, and the fabric treats it that way. The 12-bit sample arrives sign-extended and right-justified in 16 bits — note the asymmetry with TX, which is left-justified.

5

Packed into the FIFO

adc_streams / adc_controls → rx_mux → fifo_writer → rx_sample_fifo 64→32

rx_mux is the single register in the RX sample path, and it is where test sources substitute: a 12-bit ramp, a 32-bit counter, or the digital loopback FIFO fed from TX. All are explicit host commands; nothing switches automatically. The trigger gate is applied only in normal mode — counter and loopback modes bypass it entirely.

fifo_writer packs by shifting 32 bits into the top of a 64-bit word for each enabled channel, so a MIMO pair fills one word in a single call:

Mode63:4847:3231:1615:0
ch0 + ch1Q1I1Q0I0
one channelQ (newer)I (newer)Q (older)I (older)

From a valid at the fabric boundary to the word resident in the FIFO is 4 rx_clock edges. The write cadence is one word every 2 cycles in MIMO, every 4 with a single channel — and either way 1022 writes per message, mirroring the TX side exactly.

6

The metadata header

128-bit word · emitted least-significant dword first · 4 bus words

The header is recomputed combinationally every cycle from the live timestamp and registered on every edge, so whichever cycle the meta FSM chooses to write, the value is current. On the wire it reads: magic 0x12344321, timestamp low, timestamp high, flags — the timestamp little-endian across two words, flags last.

The timestamp itself is a free-running divide-by-two of rx_clock, so it increments once per sample period and is never gated on data. It is held in reset whenever metadata mode is off.

There is no RX overrun status bit

The FPGA detects the condition — and then only blinks LED3. The overflow counter output is left unconnected in rx.vhd and synthesized away. The host infers overrun purely from a discontinuity in the header timestamps, which means without the metadata format there is no overrun detection at all. The underrun bit that does appear in the RX header is a TX condition, patched in on the way out — so you need an RX stream running to observe TX underruns.

7

Home

SETUP_RD 3 · META_READ 1 · SAMPLE_READ 2044 · FINISHED 11

The return transaction is five cycles shorter than the outbound one — 2060 against 2065 — because the RX acknowledgement can be asserted later. The FIFOs are show-ahead, so the head word is already presented and no read-latency bubble is needed; header and payload run contiguously with no gap at the boundary.

Then back out through PIB_SOCKET_0, into the USB consumer socket, and up EP 0x81 to the host.

One discrepancy I could not resolve

The RX cadence is firmly established — a strobe every two l_clk cycles, cross-checked against the testbench’s own timestamp assertions. The TX side should mirror it, same framing and same clock, but the DAC rate register is programmed to DAC_RATE(3) in 2R2T LVDS mode, which makes the ADI core request data every four cycles, not two.

I could not reconcile those from source. The AD9361 clock-tree code that would settle it lives in an unfetched submodule. Practically it does not threaten correctness — the FPGA logic is rate-agnostic, emitting exactly one sample per request and reading one 64-bit word per two samples whatever the ratio — but the absolute TX sample rate is not something this repo can confirm. Comparing the TX and RX timestamp rates on hardware would settle it in a minute.

The NIOS

The soft CPU that executes every host command: what it is, every pin and peripheral it reaches, everything it does — with a worked example of each — and the surprising answer to how it starts and how it is switched off.

What it actually is

altera_nios2_gen2 · impl = Tiny · 80 MHz

A Nios II/e soft CPU — the free, licence-unencumbered variant. The build script defaults to it because Nios II/f needs Quartus Standard or Pro, and that default has a consequence most people miss: a lot of the CPU configuration in the build script is inert. The 2 KB instruction and data caches, the hardware multiplier, the barrel shifter, the SRT divider and the dynamic branch predictor are all specified in nios_system.tcl — and a Nios II/e has none of them.

So: no cache, every instruction fetched over the Avalon fabric from on-chip RAM, roughly 6 cycles per instruction, and every * or / compiled into a libgcc call. That is why the entire firmware is built -Os — smaller code is genuinely faster here, not just smaller.

It has one job: execute host command packets. It never touches a sample.

Why your board matters

The RAM size gates a #if RAM_SPAN >= 131072 that defines BLADERF_NIOS_LIBAD936X. Your A9 sits exactly at the threshold, so the full Analog Devices AD9361 driver is compiled into the NIOS and the RFIC command path is live. On an A2 it is not, and the host silently falls back to driving the RFIC itself over raw SPI.

Life and death

how it starts, and the fact that it essentially never stops

Where the program lives

There is no bootloader and no firmware download. The compiled ELF is converted by elf2hex into a memory-initialisation file, referenced through meminit.qip, and Quartus bakes it into the on-chip RAM's power-up contents. The NIOS program is part of the bitstream.

The practical consequence: changing one line of NIOS C requires a full Quartus recompile. There is no faster path, short of a JTAG download.

Nothing ever resets it

This is the single most surprising thing in the chapter. The Qsys system's reset input is tied high in the VHDL:

U_nios_system : component nios_system
    port map (
        clk_clk       => sys_clock,
        reset_reset_n => '1',          -- never asserted, ever

That port is the reset sink feeding every slave in the system, the CPU and RAM included. So after FPGA configuration the NIOS is never reset again. All of its state — RFIC register shadows, retune queues, fastlock profile tables, the trim-DAC cache — survives host connect, disconnect, bladerf_open, bladerf_close and USB re-enumeration.

The FX3's sys_reset_async line does not reach it either. That signal has exactly two uses in the whole top level: it forces the command UART's RX line to idle and tri-states its TX. The NIOS keeps running at full speed and keeps all its state — it simply stops hearing the host.

And the PLL reset only gates the clock. If the PLL loses lock the NIOS clock stops; when it relocks the CPU resumes mid-instruction-stream, with no reset.

The loop has no exit

/* on real hardware, this is not a variable */
#define run_nios true

while (run_nios) { ... }

On the target run_nios is a macro expanding to true — the symbol only exists in the PC-simulation builds. The loop body contains exactly one control-transfer statement, a continue for an unrecognised magic byte. There is no break, no return, no exit(), no abort() anywhere in the NIOS sources, and the BSP is built with hal.enable_exit 0. The return 0; at the end of main() is unreachable.

bladerf_device_reset() is worth spelling out because the mechanism is indirect. It sends a vendor request that makes the FX3 reboot. On reboot the FX3 runs its cold GPIO init, which drives nCONFIG low — de-configuring the Cyclone V and killing the NIOS — and then reloads the bitstream from flash if a valid autoload image is present. So it does restart the NIOS, through a path that never involves the NIOS at all.

No watchdog, and places it can hang

There is no timer and no watchdog instantiated anywhere in the system, no exception handler registered, and ASSERT compiles to nothing. The I²C completion routine contains three unbounded spin loops with no timeout — a stuck bus wedges the CPU permanently. The SPI arbiter lock is a fourth. A hung NIOS produces a 250 ms timeout per host access and BLADERF_ERR_TIMEOUT; libbladeRF has no retry and no recovery. Only reconfiguring the FPGA, a power cycle, or JTAG gets it back.

The pins

two levels that are easy to conflate

“The NIOS's pins” means two different things, and both matter.

Level A — the CPU's own interfaces

Inside the Qsys system the CPU exposes: clk, reset, instruction_master, data_master, irq, and debug_mem_slave. That is all. There is no MMU or MPU interface, no tightly-coupled memory ports, no external interrupt controller port, and the custom_instruction_master is unconnected — there are no custom instructions.

The instruction master reaches only two slaves: the debug module and RAM. Code executes exclusively from on-chip RAM. The data master reaches 19:

The generated nios_system component exposes 99 ports to the fabric, of which 96 are connected in the hosted build. Ports that are tied off, left open, or absent entirely:

  • reset_reset_n — tied '1'
  • Every _n half of the AD9361 LVDS interface — tied '0' or left open; Quartus infers the pair from the pin assignments
  • ad9361_dac_sync_in_sync, oc_i2c_arst_i, both tamers' ts_sync_in and ts_pps — tied '0'
  • Both tamers' ts_sync_out — open (and hardwired '0' inside the IP anyway)
  • All three arbiter ports — absent from the port map entirely
  • All ten Wishbone ports — mapped to signals nothing else drives or reads
  • gpio_rffe_0 bit 4 — a dead output; its consumer is commented out

What it does

nine packet families, with a real example of each

Everything the NIOS does happens in one of two places, and the distinction governs performance:

if (have_request) {
    handler->exec(&pkt);                 // SYNCHRONOUS — blocking, uninterruptible
    command_uart_write_response(pkt.resp);
} else {
    // VCTCXO tamer state machine
    pkt_handlers[i].do_work();             // DEFERRED — only when no packet is pending
}

Deferred work is starved by packet traffic. A host that hammers the command UART back to back stops the retune queue and the RFIC write queue from advancing at all. This is precisely why libbladeRF's RFIC spin-wait sleeps 100 µs between polls — the sleep is what lets the NIOS reach do_work().

Every packet is 16 bytes each way, dispatched on byte 0. The magic set is enforced in hardware by the command UART before the NIOS ever sees it.

A

8x8 — LMS6, Si5338, VCTCXO mode, triggers

Arm the RX trigger as master (ARM|MASTER = 0x05 into the RX trigger PIO):

41 04 01 00 00 05 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
^A ^t ^w    ^a ^d
B

8x16 — trim DAC, INA219, IQ correction

Set the AD5621 VCTCXO trim DAC to mid-scale. The NIOS clocks out exactly two bytes, 0x80 0x00, on peripheral SPI slave-select 0:

42 03 01 00 00 00 80 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
            ^lo ^hi

Readback never touches the bus — the AD5621 has no readback path, so the NIOS returns a cached copy.

C

8x32 — version, control register, fastlock, RFFE CSR

Read the FPGA version. The response decodes to v0.16.0:

→ 43 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
← 43 00 02 00 00 00 10 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
        ^success      ^data LE = 0x00001000

Force LED2 on (led_mode bit 15 plus LED bits 14:12):

43 01 01 00 00 00 A0 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00

The NIOS has no LED logic whatsoever — the LEDs are three bits of a PIO, and with led_mode clear the FPGA drives them autonomously (LED1 blinks, LED2 = TX underflow, LED3 = RX overflow).

D

8x64 — timestamps

Read the RX timestamp. One target, read-only; the NIOS performs eight successive byte reads from the tamer and assembles them LSB-first:

44 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
E

16x64 — the AD9361, both ways

This family carries two very different targets. Target 0x00 is raw SPI — read AD9361 register 0x037:

45 00 00 00 37 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
      ^read ^addr LE

Target 0x01 is the RFIC command interface, where the address packs a command and a channel. Tune RX1 to 2.400 GHz (command 0x04, channel 0):

45 01 01 00 04 00 00 18 0D 8F 00 00 00 00 00 00
      ^wr   ^addr ^--- 2400000000 LE ---^

Here is the asymmetry that matters: RFIC reads execute immediately; RFIC writes are only enqueued. The response means “accepted”, not “done”. The actual ad9361_set_rx_lo_freq() runs later in do_work(), through a three-tick state machine — one transition per main-loop pass. The host discovers completion by polling a status command, which works only because reads bypass the queue.

F

32x32 — expansion port, ADI AXI, Wishbone

Set expansion-port bits 3:0 to 0b0101 without disturbing the rest. The address field is a bitmask, and the NIOS performs the read-modify-write:

4B 00 01 00 0F 00 00 00 05 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
      ^wr   ^--- mask ---^ ^--- data ---^
G

Legacy — the FX3-era format

Kept for backward compatibility. Byte 1 is a config word packing direction, device and count. Read LMS6 register 0x2F — this is one of the repo's own verified test vectors:

→ 4e 91 2f ff 00 ...   cfg 0x91 = READ | DEV_LMS | count 1
← 4e 91 2f 17 ff ...   returned 0x17

Multi-byte parameters are assembled across successive packets using file-static accumulators — genuine cross-packet state living in the NIOS.

H

retune2 — the one Chapter 2 cares about

An immediate RX retune to stored profile 0, RFFE slot 3, highband. Timestamp zero means “now”, and the whole thing runs synchronously inside the handler:

55 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 03 80 01 00 00
^U ^------ timestamp = 0 (NOW) ------^ ^p ^rf ^port ^spdt

The NIOS then performs, in order: load the 16-byte fastlock profile into the RFFE slot if it is not already resident, one SPI write to register 0x25A to recall it, a read-modify-write of register 0x004 for port selection, and a read-modify-write of the RFFE GPIO for the SPDT switches. Any non-zero, non-sentinel timestamp instead enqueues the request and defers activation to do_work().

Assembly and toolchain

the short answer is: no assembly at all

A sweep of the entire repository — find for *.S, *.s and *.asm, greps for __asm__ and asm volatile, and greps for Nios II builtins like __builtin_wrctl and ALT_CI_* — returns zero hits. The build system even declares an empty assembly source list, and the assemble rules it carries are dead code.

The firmware is 100% C. Even the lowest-level register accessors are not inline assembly: IORD_32DIRECT and IOWR_32DIRECT expand to the GCC builtins __builtin_ldwio and __builtin_stwio, which the compiler backend emits as single cache-bypassing load/store instructions. Those headers live in the Quartus installation, not the repo.

Assembly does reach the final image, but none of it is Nuand's and none of it is checked in: Altera's generated crt0.S (the C runtime startup that clears .bss, sets up the stack and global pointer, and calls into main), the HAL's exception and IRQ entry stubs, and pre-built newlib routines.

The VHDL it drives

four Nuand IP blocks, each with a register map the firmware writes by hand

Yes — VHDL is very much involved. Four Nuand blocks sit directly on the NIOS's data bus, and the firmware talks to them through raw byte and word accesses at fixed offsets. None of these register maps is generated; they are hand-matched between the VHDL and the C.

command_uart · 0x9120 · IRQ 7

A 4 Mbaud soft UART with 128-bit request and response registers — exactly one 16-byte packet each way. Byte-addressed: offsets 0/4/8/12 are the four words of the packet, and offset 16 is a control byte whose bit 0 enables the interrupt.

Two behaviours worth knowing. Reading the request register is what clears the interrupt. And writing word 0 of the response starts transmission immediately, before words 1–3 have been written — safe only because each byte takes about 200 clocks.

time_tamer · 0x9160 (RX) / 0x9140 (TX) · IRQ 3 / 2

Offsets 0–7 read the 64-bit timestamp; reading offset 0 latches the counter so the following seven byte reads are coherent. Writing offsets 0–7 loads a compare value, and offset 8 is a command register: write 0 to arm the interrupt, write 1 to acknowledge it.

The counter increments every other ts_clock edge — one count per two sample clocks. Offset 9 exposes a status_past flag meaning “you asked for a time already gone”; the firmware never reads it, so that condition is invisible to software.

arbiter · 0x9500 · IRQ 6

A two-requester round-robin guarding AD9361 SPI. Offset 0 bit 0 requests, offset 1 bit 0 reports the grant, offset 0 bit 1 releases. The firmware wraps every AD9361 SPI access in a lock/spin/unlock — a write, an unbounded polled read, and a write, on top of each transaction.

wishbone_master · 0x10000000 · IRQ 10

A transparent Avalon-to-Wishbone address window, not a register block. See the defects section — on this image it is not functional.

Dead code and defects

found while tracing; all read directly from source

A deep read turns up a fair amount of code that cannot execute on this board, and a few genuine bugs. None of these are speculative — each was read out of the RTL or the C.

VCTCXO tamer is a stub
dead
On the micro, vctcxo_tamer_0 is instantiated as a 256-byte on-chip RAM with no interrupt, not the Nuand tamer IP. Its ISR can therefore never fire, and the entire coarse/fine VCTCXO discipline state machine in main() is unreachable dead code. On bladeRF1 the same code drives real hardware on IRQ 0.
Wishbone master is inert
bug
All ten wbm_wb_* ports map to top-level signals that nothing drives or reads — including the Wishbone clock and the acknowledge. Writes vanish; a read would park the state machine waiting for an ack that never comes, holding waitrequest high and hanging the NIOS data master indefinitely.
Scheduled timestamp MSB
bug
The tamer scheduling routine writes byte 7 as (time >> 54) where it should be >> 56, so bits 56–63 of every scheduled retune timestamp carry bits 61–54 instead. Harmless below 254 samples, but wrong.
Trim DAC, wrong part
dead
The VCTCXO tuning state machine issues the bladeRF1 DAC161S055 three-byte command format, but the micro is fitted with an AD5621 that takes two bytes. Unobservable only because the state machine never runs.
IQ and AGC correction
stub
Two 8x16 targets acknowledge with SUCCESS and do nothing — the PIOs they would drive are not instantiated on the micro, so the compiler selects empty function bodies.
Arbiter, half-wired
mixed
The fabric-facing channel is absent from the hosted port map entirely, and IRQ 6 is wired in Qsys with no ISR registered. But the NIOS's own channel is internal and live, so the lock/spin/unlock around every SPI access is real cost. The source comment saying it “applies only to bladeRF1” is wrong.
Unbounded spin loops
risk
The I²C completion routine has three untimed spins and the SPI arbiter lock a fourth. With no watchdog anywhere in the system, a stuck bus is unrecoverable short of reconfiguring the FPGA.
Stale break vector
cosmetic
The CPU's breakSlave names an instance nios2_qsys_0 that does not exist in this system — a leftover from an older system name. Inert, because Qsys resolves the break vector from the debug module regardless.

Chapter 04

The TX FIFO

The buffer between the USB bus and the radio clock, on the transmit side. What drives every port, what gates a write, and which of its flags are wired across the chip only to be ignored.

What it is

32-bit in, 64-bit out, 8192 words deep

One M10K-backed dual-clock FIFO, 32 KiB of storage, sitting between the FX3 bus and the AD9361 sample clock. Written 32 bits at a time by the GPIF interface, read 64 bits at a time by the sample reader.

The wrapper is a pure pass-through to Altera's dcfifo_mixed_widths; every generic is forwarded verbatim. Three of them shape everything downstream:

  • LPM_SHOWAHEAD = "ON" — the head word is already present on q. rdreq is an acknowledge, not a request. This is why the reader never waits a cycle for data.
  • ADD_USEDW_MSB_BIT = "OFF" — neither occupancy counter can express “completely full”; both wrap to zero. That is why the separate full flags exist, and why the RX side concatenates full & usedw before comparing.
  • OVERFLOW_CHECKING / UNDERFLOW_CHECKING = "ON" — the megafunction internally masks wrreq when full and rdreq when empty. This is the only cycle-level protection the TX write side has.

The depth is not set where you would expect. The wrapper defaults to 4096 and is overridden at instantiation to 2**(sample_fifo.wused'length) — the width of the record field, which is itself derived from TX_FIFO_LENGTH = 8192. So the single knob is TX_FIFO_LENGTH; editing the wrapper default alone does nothing.

What has to be true for a write

wrreq is asserted in exactly one state

wrreq defaults low every cycle and is set in one place only — the SAMPLE_WRITE state of the GPIF state machine. Getting there requires, in order:

  • The FX3 asserts its TX-enable line and raises a DMA request on one of the TX request pins.
  • The watermark passes: fewer than 6144 of 8192 words occupied, and the metadata FIFO below half full. Either term failing blocks the transfer before it starts.
  • Arbitration in IDLE picks TX — which it normally does, since TX is checked before a non-critical RX to avoid discontinuities in the transmitted signal.
  • If metadata is on, the header's timestamp survives a check: zero (send now) or more than 32 ticks in the future. A late buffer is discarded entirely — the FSM diverts to SAMPLE_WRITE_IGNORE, raises the underrun flag, and wrreq is never asserted for those 2048 words.

Note what is not in that list: the full flag. Once a burst starts, wrreq is unconditional for its whole length. The 2048-word headroom the watermark guarantees is the entire safety margin, backed only by the megafunction's internal masking.

And for a read

rdreq needs all of these simultaneously: the trigger-gated empty is low; the metadata FSM has reached the scheduled launch timestamp; loopback back-pressure is clear; at least one enabled DAC channel is asking for a sample; and the MIMO unpacker has exhausted the current 64-bit word. With two channels enabled that last condition is met on every request; with one channel, every other request.

The trigger is wired into the empty flag

the least obvious thing in this chapter

The FIFO's rdempty output does not go to the reader. It goes to a signal named sample_fifo_rempty_untriggered, passes through the multi-board trigger block, and only its output reaches fifo_reader:

txtrig : entity work.trigger(async)
    generic map ( DEFAULT_OUTPUT => '1' )
    port map (
        signal_in  => sample_fifo_rempty_untriggered,
        signal_out => sample_fifo.rempty
    );

Inside, it is three lines of combinational logic. While the trigger is armed and has not fired, rempty is forced high — the reader believes the FIFO is permanently dry while data keeps accumulating behind it. That is how a triggered transmit holds without touching the FIFO's real state.

Two consequences

An armed-but-unfired TX lights the underflow LED continuously, because the underflow detector also watches this gated flag. And the metadata FIFO's empty flag is not gated this way — it goes straight through. If you assume the two FIFOs behave symmetrically here, you will be wrong.

Wired but never read

signals that cost routing and do nothing

wrfull, wrempty
dead
Both are routed from the FIFO, through tx.vhd, across the entity boundary into fx3_gpif — where each appears exactly once, in its own port declaration. Four signals crossing two boundaries to be discarded. The RX side does read its full flag, so this is a TX-only oversight, not a convention.

dead
Connected to a record field that appears once in the whole file — the port map line itself. Driven, never read, never leaves the module.

dead
Wired into fifo_reader, but the body never references the value. Only its 'length is used — to size the very port that goes unread. fifo_reader is driven purely by the empty flag.

note
The tx_fifo_t record describes one FIFO's whole interface, but the design splits that FIFO across two files. Each file leaves the other's half at its default, so roughly half of every declared record instance is constant-folded away. Nothing breaks; it just means the record is a naming convention, not a bundle.

Where the data comes from

one 32-bit word, tracked

Before it arrives:

host app → libbladeRF → USB 3.0 bulk OUT → FX3 GPIF II
  → fx3_gpif[31:0] pins → register_gpif (1 pclk reg)
  → fx3_gpif.gpif_mux (1 more pclk reg) → tx_fifo

After it leaves:

tx_fifo → CDC to tx_clock → fifo_reader (unpack + timestamp gate)
  → dac_assignment_proc (truncate to 12 bits, left-justified)
  → axi_ad9361 → LVDS → AD9361 DAC → TX1 / TX2

The metadata rides a parallel track for part of that journey only. It enters fx3_gpif, is shifted into a 128-bit buffer, dribbles into the meta FIFO during the sample writes rather than before them, and is consumed by the reader's metadata FSM — where it never becomes samples. It only decides when the sample FSM is allowed to pop.

The RX FIFO

The mirror image on the receive side — and the three places it deliberately is not a mirror. Its occupancy count is what reaches back into bus arbitration and pre-empts transmit.

What it is

64-bit in, 32-bit out, 8192 words deep — the mirror of Chapter 4, with three real differences

Same wrapper, same megafunction, opposite direction: 64 KiB of M10K, written by the sample packer in the AD9361 clock domain and drained 32 bits at a time by the GPIF interface. Twice the storage of the TX FIFO, because the same 8192-word depth now counts 64-bit words.

Three things genuinely differ from TX, and all three matter:

  • The occupancy counters have different widths — 13 bits on the write side, 14 on the read side. Same physical buffer, counted in different units.
  • The full flags are actually read. Both sides consult them, and both concatenate full & usedw to work around the counters' inability to express “completely full”.
  • The clear line is different. TX is cleared only by a global reset; RX is flushed every time the host disables RX.

The two waterlines

both derive from rdusedw, and they do different jobs

1

rx_fifo_enough — may a transfer start?

Compares rdfull & rdusedw — a 15-bit value — against the GPIF buffer size. It trips once 2048 32-bit words (8 KiB, one whole DMA buffer) are readable on SuperSpeed, 1024 on High Speed.

It is one of four AND terms in can_rx. False means the state machine never leaves IDLE toward a read: no rdreq, no DMA acknowledge, nothing sent to the host. In packet mode this sample count is replaced entirely by “a packet header exists”.

2

rx_fifo_critical — is RX about to overflow?

Compares bare rdusedw (no full flag) against half the read-side capacity minus one buffer — tripping at 6144 words on SuperSpeed.

This is the signal that reaches back into bus arbitration, and it is the reason Chapter 2's half-duplex bus behaves the way it does. Normally TX is checked first, deliberately, to avoid discontinuities in the transmitted signal. But a critical RX is checked before both — so a nearly-full RX FIFO pre-empts a pending TX transfer. It is the anti-overflow escape hatch.

The comment and the arithmetic disagree

The source calls this “can we not fit one more block of data in RX buffer?”, but the expression measures against half the read-side depth. The read side holds 16384 words, so “critical” actually fires at 37.5% full, not one block from full. The TX equivalent has no such halving. Conservative rather than wrong — but if you are reasoning about headroom, use the number, not the comment.

The clear line, and a crossing that is waived rather than synchronized

worth knowing if you ever chase an intermittent RX fault

Unlike TX, the RX FIFO is flushed on every RX disable. The chain is short:

FX3 asserts CTL[4]  (dma_rx_enable)
  → sampled by a single flop in fx3_gpif → rx_enable_pclk
  → inverted, straight into aclr — no synchronizer

rx_enable_pclk lives in the PCLK domain, which is the FIFO's read clock. Its assertion edge is therefore synchronous to the read side but fully asynchronous to the write side, where the sample clock runs. There is no RTL synchronizer on that path. De-assertion is handled inside the megafunction; the assertion edge is covered by explicit timing waivers instead:

set_false_path -from {fx3_gpif*rx_enable} -to {*rx_fifo*:wraclr|*}

By contrast the enable that gates the writer is properly three-flop synchronized before use. So: the writer's enable crosses safely; the FIFO's async clear crosses by constraint waiver. Both are defensible, but they are not the same mechanism, and only one of them is visible in the RTL.

The sibling FIFOs

two more, sharing the same control structure

rx_meta_fifo — 128 in, 32 out, 2048 deep

Carries the timestamp headers alongside the samples. Cleared by the same NOT rx_enable_pclk line, deliberately, so metadata and samples can never desynchronize. Its header is built as a 128-bit word and read out as four 32-bit slices; the last slice has its low 16 bits overwritten on the way out with the TX underrun flags.

Only one of its flags does any work: the read-side rdempty, which gates rx_meta_ok in the transfer-start decision. Its rdfull and read-side occupancy are declared in fx3_gpif and never referenced — and note the port is spelled usedr, not usedw, unlike every sibling.

A misleading comment

The depth constant is annotated “2048 -- 32-bit words”. It is not. LPM_NUMWORDS is a write-side count, so it is 2048 × 128-bit entries = 8192 32-bit words. The firmware reads it correctly; the comment does not.

lb_fifo — 64 in, 64 out, 512 deep

The digital loopback path, and the only one of the three that runs TX → RX: written in the TX domain from the words popped off the TX FIFO, read in the RX domain. Because both widths are equal it elaborates a plain dcfifo rather than the mixed-width variant.

Its write-full flag is the one piece of genuine back-pressure in the system: it reaches back into tx.vhd and stalls the TX reader for three cycles so loopback data is not dropped. Its read side is pulled only when the previous word is fully unpacked and the RX sample FIFO has room.

Curiously, both its clocks alias the same net on this board, so it is physically synchronous while being parameterized asynchronous — costing latency and gray-code logic to keep the wrapper uniform. It is also the only one of the three whose clear is properly resynchronized in RTL, which is just as well: it is the one the timing waivers do not cover.

If metadata is on, four header dwords are emitted ahead of the samples in the same transfer, and the last of them carries the TX underrun flag. That is the only channel by which a transmit-side problem reaches the host — which is why an RX stream has to be running to observe one.

How this was made

Method, and its limits

Everything is traced from the bladeRF source at revision 35174c30: the VHDL and Verilog, the Qsys build script, the FX3 firmware, and the .qip build manifests, which are authoritative for what is actually compiled in. Cycle counts are read out of the state machines. Clock rates are derived from the timing constraints and the deserializer geometry.

Nothing here is measured on hardware. Where the source could not settle a question — the true GPIF clock, the AD9361 clock-tree ratios, anything living in the unfetched no-OS submodule — the chapters say so explicitly rather than guessing.
